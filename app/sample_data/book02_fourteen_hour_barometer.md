## Chapter 1: The Meniscus Falls

The mercury in the Torricellian column had dropped forty-four millimeters in ninety minutes, and Ronan knew the basalt cliffs would not hold. He wiped the brine off the glass tube with the sleeve of his heavy oilskin smock and checked the vernier scale for the fourth time since midnight.

Outside the lighthouse dome, the Atlantic was tossing six-ton blocks of dolerite across the landing slip like pebbles.

**RONAN: THURSDAY, 08:00 TELEMETRY LOG**
$\Delta P / \Delta t = -2.93 \text{ hPa/hr}$ | $T_{\text{ambient}} = 3.4^\circ\text{C}$ | Barometric Gradient: Extreme
Meniscus curvature: Inverted convex (-0.82mm) indicating subsurface piezoelectric surge.
Station 04 (Ghyll Skerries) isolation confirmed. VLF carrier signal lost at 07:44 GMT.

"The relay broke three miles off the south horn," Martha shouted over the thrumming roar of the lantern mantle. She dropped a bundle of wet charts onto the slate table, her hair dripping cold seafoam across the ink lines.

"It didn't break from the gale," Ronan said without lifting his eyes from the brass dial. "The strain gauges on the seafloor cable registered eight hundred tons of tension before the copper snapped. That isn't wave action, Martha. That's tectonic displacement."

She pulled an oilcloth wrap from her pack and laid a flat piece of dark stone onto the dry corner of the chart desk. It was an irregular tablet of Welsh basalt, chipped along the bevel, its polished face scratched with jagged white chalk marks.

:::welsh-slate
DO NOT VENTILATE THE LOWER GALLERY.
THE AIR PRESSURE INSIDE THE SHAFT IS HOLDING BACK THE WATER TABLE.
IF THE BAROMETER FALLS BELOW 960 HPA, SEAL THE VAULT FROM OUTSIDE.
— GRIFFITH (LIGHTKEEPER FIRST CLASS)
:::

Ronan traced the chalk groove with the tip of his thumb. The white dust came away dry on his skin—calcite and ground gypsum from the deeper seams beneath the island.

"Griffith was down in the gallery three days ago," Ronan said. "Before the storm even showed on the Admiralty charts. He already knew."

***

The vibration came not through the air, but through the three-foot granite floor under their boots. The pendulum clock on the timber wall stuttered, its anchor escapement missing two teeth in quick succession, before the bronze bob jerked sideways and struck the glass cabinet.

"Eight hours left," Martha said, checking her pocket watch against the dead dial. "At fourteen hours, the tide reaches the upper cavern mouth. If the pressure doesn't stabilize, the siphon effect will pull the entire lighthouse into the channel."

Ronan unscrewed the brass safety valve of the reserve air pump. "Then we don't wait for Griffith's tide. We balance the shaft now."

## Chapter 2: The Siphon at Low Ebb

The spiral stairs dropped two hundred feet into the heart of the basalt plug. Each iron riser rang beneath their boots like a struck chime, sending dull echoes down into the boiling darkness where the surge pipe met the open Atlantic.

The air in the lower gallery smelled of ionized ozone and wet pumice. Ronan held his carbide lamp forward. The yellow flame guttered, turning a ghostly pale green as the hydrocarbon vapors seeped from the fissures.

"Keep the flame behind the wire mesh," Martha whispered, her hand tightening on the pressure wrench.

Before them rose the iron bulkhead of the compensation chamber, its twenty-four dog-latches bolted tight against the dark weight of the sea.
